# Simulation Study

We conduct an extensive simulation study to validate our theoretical results and illustrate the three regimes of federated learning identified in Section 4. All experiments use the data-generating process described in Section 2. We begin by describing the common simulation setup, then present results for ridgeless and ridge estimators, followed by an investigation of the role of heterogeneity.

## Simulation Setup

We fix $n = 2000$ total training observations split equally across $k = 2$ clusters ($n_1 = n_2 = 1000$). The coefficient vector $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, I_p)$ and normalized to unit norm ($\|\beta\| = 1$). For cluster $b \in \{1, 2\}$, training data is generated as $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_b, \Sigma_b)$ with response $y_i = x_i^\top \beta + \varepsilon_i$, where $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. Unless otherwise noted, we set $\sigma = 1/\sqrt{5}$ so that $\text{SNR} = \|\beta\|^2 / \sigma^2 = 5$, matching the highest signal-to-noise ratio considered by Hastie et al. (2022). Test data ($n_{\text{test}} = 1000$) is drawn from the same mixture distribution as training. All results are averaged over 20 independent trials with 95\% confidence intervals reported.

The cluster means $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ are unit-norm random vectors whose angle $\theta$ and norm ratio $c = \|\mu_2\| / \|\mu_1\|$ parameterize the degree of heterogeneity between clusters. Our default configuration uses $\theta = 60°$ and $c = 1$ (equal norms). Covariance matrices are $\Sigma_1 = \Sigma_2 = I_p$ unless otherwise specified.

The **federated learner** fits a separate model on each cluster and averages predictions: $\hat{y}_{\text{fed}} = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{y}_1 + \hat{y}_2)$. The **centralized learner** fits a single model on the pooled data. For linear models, prediction averaging is equivalent to parameter averaging (as in FedAvg), since $\frac{1}{2}(x^\top \hat{\beta}_1 + x^\top \hat{\beta}_2) = x^\top \frac{1}{2}(\hat{\beta}_1 + \hat{\beta}_2)$. Our primary metric is the **MSE ratio**: $R = \text{MSE}_{\text{fed}} / \text{MSE}_{\text{merged}}$, where $R < 1$ indicates federation wins and $R > 1$ indicates centralization wins.


## Ridgeless Regression: Sweeping Across the Interpolation Threshold

Our main experiment sweeps $p \in \{250, 500, 750, 1050, 1500, 2050, 3000, 4000, 5000, 6000, 7000, 8000\}$ with the ridgeless (minimum-norm least squares) estimator. This range spans three critical transitions: the cluster interpolation threshold at $p = n/k = 1000$, the merged interpolation threshold at $p = n = 2000$, and the deep overparameterized regime. We avoid $p$ exactly equal to $n/k$ or $n$ to prevent numerical instability at the singular boundary.

**Figure [ridgeless_ratio]** displays the MSE ratio as a function of $p$. Three regimes are clearly visible:

*Regime I: Both underparameterized ($p < 1000$).* The MSE ratio increases from $1.17$ at $p = 250$ ($\gamma_{\text{global}} = 0.125$) to $2.50$ at $p = 750$ ($\gamma_{\text{global}} = 0.375$), confirming that centralization consistently outperforms federation when both learners are in the classical regime. As $p$ approaches the cluster threshold, the advantage of centralization grows because cluster models suffer from increasing variance while the merged model, with twice the data, remains well-conditioned.

*Regime II: Staggered ($1000 < p < 2000$).* This regime exhibits the most dramatic effects. At $p = 1050$, just past the cluster interpolation threshold, the MSE ratio spikes to $9.52$: the federated learner's test MSE is nearly an order of magnitude worse than the centralized learner's. Each cluster model, with $\gamma_{\text{local}} = 1.05$, is at the interpolation catastrophe — perfectly fitting its training data including noise — while the merged model ($\gamma_{\text{global}} = 0.525$) remains safely underparameterized. By $p = 1500$, the cluster models have moved past the peak ($\gamma_{\text{local}} = 1.5$) and the ratio drops to $0.73$, meaning federation now outperforms centralization. The cluster models have entered the regime of benign overfitting while the merged model approaches its own threshold.

*Regime III: Both overparameterized ($p > 2000$).* At $p = 2050$, the merged model is at its interpolation threshold ($\gamma_{\text{global}} = 1.025$) while the cluster models have $\gamma_{\text{local}} = 2.05$. The MSE ratio plummets to $0.062$: the federated learner's MSE is approximately 16 times lower than the centralized learner's. This is the most striking result — federation is overwhelmingly superior precisely because the centralized model is experiencing the interpolation catastrophe while the federated models have already recovered. As $p$ increases further, the merged model recovers and the ratio converges toward 1 from below, crossing 1.0 near $p = 5000$ ($\gamma_{\text{global}} = 2.5$). In the deep overparameterized regime ($p = 8000$, $\gamma_{\text{global}} = 4.0$), the ratio is $1.02$ — the two approaches are essentially equivalent, with centralization holding a negligible edge.

**Figure [ridgeless_raw_mse]** shows the raw test MSEs of both learners alongside the null risk ($\text{MSE}_{\text{null}} = \|y_{\text{test}}\|^2 / n_{\text{test}} \approx 1.0$). Both learners' MSEs approach the null risk as $p \to \infty$, consistent with the asymptotic theory of Hastie et al. (2022), which predicts $R(\gamma) \to r^2$ from below as $\gamma \to \infty$ when $\text{SNR} > 1$. The interpolation spikes at $p = 1050$ (cluster models) and $p = 2050$ (merged model) are clearly visible as transient peaks in the respective MSE curves.


## Ridge Regression: Regularization Eliminates the Federation Advantage

We repeat the identical experiment using ridge regression with leave-one-out cross-validated regularization. The penalty parameter $\lambda$ is selected from a grid of 20 values spanning $[10^{-6}, 10^{6}]$ on a logarithmic scale, tuned independently for each learner (each cluster model and the merged model choose their own $\lambda$).

**Figure [ridge_ratio]** shows the MSE ratio across the same values of $p$. The contrast with the ridgeless case is stark: the ratio remains between $1.06$ and $1.61$ for all values of $p$, with centralization winning uniformly. There is no crossover — federation never outperforms centralization when regularization is properly tuned.

At low $p$ ($\gamma_{\text{global}} = 0.125$), the ridge ratio ($1.16$) is nearly identical to the ridgeless ratio ($1.17$), as expected: regularization has little effect in the strongly underparameterized regime. The key difference emerges near the interpolation thresholds. At $p = 1050$, where the ridgeless ratio spiked to $9.52$, the ridge ratio is only $1.57$ — ridge regression smooths over the cluster interpolation catastrophe. At $p = 2050$, where the ridgeless ratio dropped to $0.062$ (federation winning decisively), the ridge ratio is $1.34$ — ridge smooths over the merged interpolation catastrophe as well.

In the deep overparameterized regime, the ridge ratio slowly declines from $1.22$ at $p = 3000$ to $1.06$ at $p = 8000$. The tuned $\lambda$ values increase substantially with $p$: the merged model's $\lambda$ grows from $38$ at $p = 250$ to $1608$ at $p = 8000$, reflecting the need for stronger regularization as the model becomes more overparameterized.

The conclusion is clear: **the regime in which federation outperforms centralization exists only because of the interpolation catastrophe, and regularization eliminates this catastrophe**. With properly tuned ridge regression, more data always helps, and centralization is uniformly preferable from a purely statistical standpoint.


## The Role of Heterogeneity

We next investigate how the degree of heterogeneity between clusters affects the MSE ratio. We consider three axes of heterogeneity: directional divergence (angle between cluster means), signal strength imbalance (norm ratio), and covariance structure.

### Directional Heterogeneity

We fix $p = 3000$, $n = 2000$, and ridge regression, and vary the angle $\theta$ between $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ from $0°$ (identical means) to $90°$ (orthogonal means). **Figure [angle]** shows that the MSE ratio is essentially flat across all angles, ranging from $1.09$ to $1.11$. The 95\% confidence intervals overlap for all values of $\theta$, indicating no statistically significant effect of directional heterogeneity on the relative performance of federation and centralization.

### Signal Strength Heterogeneity

We fix $\theta = 30°$ and vary the norm ratio $c = \|\mu_2\| / \|\mu_1\|$ from $1$ (equal signal strength) to $10$ (one cluster's mean is 10 times the other's). **Figure [norm_ratio]** shows that while the point estimate of the MSE ratio increases from $1.10$ at $c = 1$ to $1.92$ at $c = 10$, the variance also grows dramatically: the 95\% CI at $c = 10$ spans $[0.21, 3.64]$. The large variance at high norm ratios reflects the instability of the cluster model trained on the high-norm cluster, whose features are dominated by the mean structure. Importantly, the median ratio remains close to 1 across all norm ratios — the apparent increase in the mean is driven by heavy-tailed outliers, not a systematic shift.

### Covariance Heterogeneity

We examine two forms of covariance heterogeneity. First, we replace the isotropic covariance $\Sigma = I$ with cluster-specific block-diagonal covariance matrices. Each cluster's covariance has three randomly chosen blocks of size $p/3$, with within-block correlation $\rho_b$ drawn from $\text{Uniform}(0, \rho_{\max})$, and the block assignments differ across clusters. We vary $\rho_{\max} \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9\}$. The MSE ratio ranges from $1.54$ to $1.61$ with no systematic trend, indicating that the degree of within-cluster correlation does not meaningfully affect the federation-centralization comparison.

Second, we replace the identity covariance with a heterogeneous diagonal covariance where each diagonal entry is drawn independently from $\text{Uniform}(1, 5)$, with separate draws for each cluster. This creates features with differing variances across clusters — a natural model for settings where different clients measure the same variables at different scales. The full-sweep results with this covariance structure (**Figure [hetvar_ratio]**) show the same three-regime pattern as the isotropic case, with the interpolation threshold effects preserved. The most notable difference is at $p = 2050$: the MSE ratio drops to $0.15$ (compared to $0.062$ with isotropic covariance), and in the overparameterized regime the ratios are slightly higher (e.g., $1.23$ vs. $0.83$ at $p = 3000$). The heterogeneous covariance amplifies the advantage of centralization in the deep overparameterized regime, but does not alter the regime structure.

### Summary

Across all heterogeneity dimensions, the qualitative story is unchanged: the overparameterization ratio determines the regime, and heterogeneity modulates the magnitude within each regime. This finding is robust to the choice of heterogeneity axis, its magnitude, and the covariance structure.

# Related Work

Our work sits at the intersection of three active research areas: federated learning under heterogeneity, high-dimensional regression theory, and ensemble methods in overparameterized models.

**Federated learning and heterogeneity.** The federated learning paradigm was introduced by McMahan et al. (2017), who proposed FedAvg for communication-efficient distributed training. Zhao et al. (2018) provided early empirical evidence that non-IID data can severely degrade federated performance, observing accuracy reductions of up to 55\% on highly skewed distributions. This motivated a substantial body of work on heterogeneity-robust algorithms. FedProx (Li et al., 2020a) adds a proximal term to each client's local objective to limit drift from the global model. SCAFFOLD (Karimireddy et al., 2020) introduces control variates to correct client drift, achieving convergence rates that are independent of the degree of heterogeneity. FedNova (Wang et al., 2020) addresses objective inconsistency arising from unequal local computation. Li et al. (2020b) analyzed the convergence of FedAvg on non-IID data, showing that convergence rates include residual error terms proportional to heterogeneity. The common thread is an emphasis on developing optimization methods robust to distributional differences across clients. Our work complements this line of research by showing that, from a statistical perspective, heterogeneity is a second-order concern in high dimensions — the overparameterization ratio is the dominant factor.

**Personalization and clustered federated learning.** An alternative approach to heterogeneity is to abandon the single global model and learn personalized or cluster-specific models. Fallah et al. (2020) frame personalized FL as meta-learning, using MAML to find a global initialization adaptable to each client. Mansour et al. (2020) analyze three personalization strategies — user clustering, data interpolation, and model interpolation — providing generalization bounds for each. Ghosh et al. (2020) propose IFCA, which alternates between estimating cluster identities and optimizing per-cluster models, with convergence guarantees for strongly convex losses. Our setup is related to clustered FL in that we assume known cluster assignments and train per-cluster models. However, our focus is not on the algorithmic problem of discovering clusters, but on the statistical question of when per-cluster training (federation) outperforms pooling (centralization), as a function of model complexity and heterogeneity.

**Distributed statistical estimation.** The statistical efficiency of distributed estimation under communication constraints has been studied by Zhang et al. (2013), who showed that a simple averaging estimator achieves MSE decaying as $O(N^{-1} + (N/m)^{-2})$ for $m$ machines with $N$ total samples, matching centralized rates when $m \leq \sqrt{N}$. Duchi et al. (2013) established information-theoretic lower bounds for distributed estimation, characterizing the fundamental cost of distributing data. These results concern the communication-accuracy tradeoff in the classical (underparameterized) regime. Our work extends this line of inquiry to the overparameterized regime, where the relationship between distributed and centralized estimation is qualitatively different due to the interpolation threshold.

**Double descent and overparameterized regression.** Belkin et al. (2019) identified the double descent phenomenon, and Nakkiran et al. (2020) demonstrated it in modern deep learning architectures. Hastie et al. (2022) provided exact asymptotic risk characterizations for ridgeless least squares in the proportional regime, establishing the risk formula $R(\gamma) = r^2(1 - 1/\gamma) + \sigma^2/(\gamma - 1)$ for $\gamma > 1$ with isotropic features. Bartlett et al. (2020) gave finite-sample conditions for benign overfitting in linear regression, showing that interpolation can be harmless when the covariance has many small but non-negligible eigenvalues. Our theoretical results build on this literature by extending the risk characterization to the federated setting, where two learners with different effective overparameterization ratios are compared.

**Ensembles and subagging in overparameterized models.** Most directly related to our work is the line of research on bagging and subagging in overparameterized linear models. Patil et al. (2023) characterized the risk of bagged and subagged ridgeless predictors, showing that subagging can monotonize the double descent curve and that optimal subagging achieves risk comparable to optimally-tuned ridge. Du et al. (2023) proved that the risk of the optimal full ridgeless ensemble matches that of the optimal ridge predictor, establishing a precise equivalence between ensembling and regularization. Our federated setting can be viewed as a form of structured subagging where the partition is determined by cluster membership rather than random subsampling. Our finding that heterogeneity has a second-order effect suggests that the risk properties of random-split ensembles carry over to structured partitions, at least qualitatively.

**Double descent in federated learning.** Rakovic et al. (2023) study the double descent phenomenon in the federated setting, deriving closed-form expressions for the mean excess risk in linear regression and confirming the presence of double descent under federation. They observe that federated learning can outperform centralized learning in specific overparameterization regimes. Our work differs in several respects: we provide a systematic characterization across multiple heterogeneity dimensions (directional divergence, signal strength, covariance structure), we directly compare ridgeless and ridge estimators to isolate the role of regularization, and we frame our contribution around the finding that heterogeneity is a second-order effect — a claim that requires the multi-dimensional heterogeneity sweep that is the core of our simulation study.

# Discussion (Simulation-Related Excerpts)

## Implications for Federated Learning Practice

Our simulation results yield several concrete recommendations for federated learning practitioners.

**Model sizing is the primary lever.** The most consequential design choice in federated learning is not the aggregation algorithm but the model complexity relative to the local sample size. Our results identify a simple rule: compute the local overparameterization ratio $\gamma_{\text{local}} = p / n_{\text{local}}$ and the global ratio $\gamma_{\text{global}} = p / n_{\text{total}}$. If $\gamma_{\text{local}} < 1$ (underparameterized locally), centralization is always preferable. If $\gamma_{\text{global}} \approx 1$ (near the global interpolation threshold), federation provides a substantial advantage by implicitly pushing each learner deeper into the overparameterized regime where benign overfitting operates. If $\gamma_{\text{global}} \gg 1$ (deep overparameterization), the choice barely matters.

**Regularization subsumes heterogeneity-robust aggregation.** Our comparison of ridge and ridgeless estimators reveals that the federation advantage exists only in the absence of proper regularization. With optimally-tuned ridge regression, centralization wins across all regimes, all overparameterization ratios, and all levels of heterogeneity we tested. This suggests that the practical challenge is not developing better aggregation schemes but ensuring adequate regularization — a simpler and more universal intervention. In the federated setting, regularization can be implemented locally (each client adds a ridge penalty) without requiring any changes to the aggregation protocol.

**Heterogeneity is not the bottleneck.** Our systematic sweep across directional divergence ($\theta \in [0°, 90°]$), signal strength imbalance ($c \in [1, 10]$), within-cluster correlation ($\rho_{\max} \in [0.1, 0.9]$), and covariance heterogeneity reveals that none of these heterogeneity parameters change which strategy wins. The MSE ratio varied by less than $0.03$ across all angles tested, and the block covariance correlation had similarly negligible effect. This stands in contrast to the FL literature's emphasis on non-IID robustness: algorithms such as FedProx (Li et al., 2020a), SCAFFOLD (Karimireddy et al., 2020), and FedNova (Wang et al., 2020) are specifically designed to handle heterogeneity, yet our results suggest that in high-dimensional linear models, heterogeneity is a second-order concern.

**Why FedAvg works.** A persistent empirical puzzle in federated learning is that simple FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) often performs comparably to more sophisticated algorithms despite its lack of explicit heterogeneity correction. Our results offer a statistical explanation: if the dominant factor is the overparameterization ratio rather than heterogeneity, then algorithms that address only heterogeneity provide limited marginal benefit. FedAvg succeeds not because heterogeneity is absent in practice, but because it is not the first-order source of error.

## Connection to the Double Descent Literature

Our full-sweep experiments provide a novel lens on the double descent phenomenon. The classical double descent curve (Belkin et al., 2019; Hastie et al., 2022) describes how the risk of a single estimator varies with $\gamma = p/n$. In the federated setting, we observe an *interleaved double descent*: the federated and centralized learners experience their interpolation peaks at different values of $p$ (at $p = n/k$ and $p = n$, respectively), creating a region between the peaks where their relative performance inverts. This interleaving is a structural consequence of data splitting and would occur for any ensemble method that partitions the training data.

The connection to Patil et al. (2023) is instructive. They showed that subagging (subsample aggregation with random splits) can monotonize the risk curve, eliminating the double descent peak. Our federated setting differs in that the data partition is not random but structured by cluster membership, and the subpopulations have heterogeneous distributions. Our finding that heterogeneity has a second-order effect suggests that the risk-monotonization benefits of subagging extend to structured (non-random) partitions, at least in the linear model setting.

The raw MSE curves in our full-sweep experiments closely match the theoretical risk formula of Hastie et al. (2022): $R(\gamma) = r^2(1 - 1/\gamma) + \sigma^2/(\gamma - 1)$ for $\gamma > 1$. At $\text{SNR} = 5$, the theory predicts a local minimum at $\gamma^* = \sqrt{\text{SNR}} / (\sqrt{\text{SNR}} - 1) \approx 1.81$, and our merged model achieves its best overparameterized performance near $\gamma_{\text{global}} = 2.0$ ($p = 4000$), consistent with this prediction.

# Limitations and Future Directions

Our results are established for high-dimensional linear models with Gaussian features, and several important questions remain open regarding the scope and applicability of our findings.

**Linearity and Gaussian assumptions.** The most significant limitation is the restriction to linear models. Modern federated learning primarily involves deep neural networks, and the extent to which our findings transfer to nonlinear models is an open question. In the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime, wide neural networks behave like kernel regression in a high-dimensional feature space, and the effective overparameterization ratio is governed by the network width relative to the sample size. Our results would apply directly in this regime, predicting that the choice between federated and centralized training depends primarily on the network width relative to local and global sample sizes. However, feature learning in finite-width networks — the regime where deep learning is most successful — may introduce qualitatively different effects. In particular, the ability of deep networks to learn representations that align with the data structure could make heterogeneity more consequential than our linear analysis suggests. Empirical validation on deep learning benchmarks is an important next step.

**Number and size of clients.** We have restricted attention to $k = 2$ clusters with equal sizes. Realistic federated settings involve many more clients (often hundreds or thousands), and client datasets are typically highly imbalanced. Increasing $k$ shifts the cluster interpolation threshold to $p = n/k$, which could be very small when $k$ is large, potentially placing most practical settings in the staggered or doubly-overparameterized regime. Unequal cluster sizes would create asymmetric interpolation thresholds across clients, so that some clients interpolate while others do not — introducing additional complexity that our equal-split analysis does not capture. Extending the theory and simulations to larger, heterogeneous populations of clients is a natural direction.

**Aggregation protocol.** Our federated learner uses simple one-shot prediction averaging: each client trains independently and predictions are averaged once. In practice, federated algorithms such as FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) perform multiple rounds of communication, with clients executing several local gradient steps before averaging *parameters* (not predictions) with the server. While for linear models these are equivalent (prediction averaging and parameter averaging coincide), the iterative nature of FedAvg can interact with the overparameterization ratio in ways not captured by our one-shot framework. In particular, multiple local steps can amplify client drift (Li et al., 2020b; Karimireddy et al., 2020), potentially making heterogeneity more relevant in the optimization dynamics even if it is second-order in the statistical limit. Extending our analysis to multi-round federated optimization is an important direction.

**Forms of heterogeneity.** We parameterize heterogeneity through the cluster means (angle and norm ratio) and covariance matrices. While these capture the first two moments of the feature distribution, real-world heterogeneity can manifest in other ways: differences in the conditional distribution $P(y \mid x)$ (concept drift), label distribution skew, feature space differences, or quantity skew (Zhao et al., 2018). Our linear model with shared $\beta$ across clusters rules out concept drift by construction. Settings where the true model differs across clients (i.e., each cluster has its own $\beta_b$) would fundamentally change the analysis, as the merged model would face model misspecification. This is the setting where personalization methods (Fallah et al., 2020; Ghosh et al., 2020) are most naturally motivated, and our results do not apply in this case.

**Regularization in practice.** Our comparison of ridgeless and ridge estimators shows that regularization eliminates the regime where federation outperforms centralization. However, this assumes access to optimally-tuned regularization. In federated settings, tuning the regularization parameter is itself challenging: local datasets may be too small for reliable cross-validation, and the optimal $\lambda$ may differ across clients. If regularization is poorly tuned — or if practitioners default to unregularized or weakly-regularized models — the interpolation threshold effects we identify become practically relevant. Understanding the interaction between suboptimal regularization and the federation-centralization tradeoff is a practically important open question.

**Privacy considerations.** Our analysis is purely statistical and does not account for privacy constraints. In practice, the primary motivation for federation is often not statistical efficiency but data privacy. Even in regimes where centralization is statistically superior, it may be infeasible due to regulatory, legal, or trust constraints. Our results should be interpreted as characterizing the *statistical cost or benefit* of federation, separate from its privacy value. A complete practical recommendation would need to jointly optimize the statistical and privacy tradeoffs.
