# Introduction (Draft v1)

Federated learning (FL) enables multiple clients to collaboratively train a shared model without centralizing their data (McMahan et al., 2017). A central challenge in FL is *statistical heterogeneity*: client data distributions often differ substantially, and this non-IID structure can degrade the performance of federated algorithms relative to centralized training. A large and growing body of work has sought to address this challenge through increasingly sophisticated aggregation and optimization schemes. FedProx (Li et al., 2020a) adds a proximal regularization term to prevent local models from drifting too far from the global model. SCAFFOLD (Karimireddy et al., 2020) introduces control variates to correct for client drift, achieving convergence rates independent of the degree of heterogeneity. FedNova (Wang et al., 2020) addresses objective inconsistency arising from unequal local computation across clients. Personalization approaches such as Per-FedAvg (Fallah et al., 2020) use meta-learning to find a global initialization that can be rapidly adapted to each client's distribution.

The common thread in this research agenda is the premise that data heterogeneity is the primary obstacle to effective federated learning, and that overcoming it requires algorithms specifically designed to be robust to distributional differences across clients. But is heterogeneity really the first-order concern? In this paper, we argue that in high-dimensional settings — where the number of model parameters $p$ is comparable to or exceeds the number of observations $n$ — the answer is no. The dominant factor governing whether federated learning helps or hurts, relative to centralized training, is not the degree of heterogeneity across clients, but rather the *overparameterization ratio*: the relationship between model complexity and the amount of data available to each learner.

Our argument builds on recent advances in high-dimensional statistics that have transformed our understanding of overparameterized models. Belkin et al. (2019) identified the *double descent* phenomenon, in which test risk exhibits a second descent beyond the classical bias-variance peak as model complexity grows past the interpolation threshold. Nakkiran et al. (2020) demonstrated this effect empirically across modern deep learning architectures. Hastie et al. (2022) provided a precise asymptotic characterization of the risk of ridgeless (minimum-norm) least squares in the proportional regime where $p/n \to \gamma$, establishing exact formulas for the bias-variance decomposition as a function of $\gamma$. Bartlett et al. (2020) identified conditions under which interpolation can be *benign*, providing finite-sample bounds on the excess risk of the minimum-norm interpolant. More recently, Patil et al. (2023) characterized the risk of bagged and subagged predictors in overparameterized linear models, showing that subagging can monotonize the risk curve and that optimal subagging achieves risk comparable to optimally-tuned ridge regression.

These results establish a detailed understanding of how the overparameterization ratio $\gamma = p/n$ governs prediction risk for a single learner. However, the federated learning setting introduces a crucial structural feature: when data is distributed across $k$ clients, the *local* overparameterization ratio $\gamma_{\text{local}} = p / (n/k)$ differs from the *global* ratio $\gamma_{\text{global}} = p/n$ by a factor of $k$. This means that local models and the centralized model necessarily occupy different positions on the double descent curve. When $p$ falls between $n/k$ and $n$, local models have crossed the interpolation threshold while the centralized model has not — or vice versa. This *staggering of interpolation thresholds* creates regimes in which federation can be either dramatically better or dramatically worse than centralization, driven not by heterogeneity but by the statistical mechanics of high-dimensional estimation.

We formalize this observation in the setting of high-dimensional linear regression with clustered data. Each of $k$ clients holds data from a distinct multivariate Gaussian distribution, and the response follows a standard linear model with additive noise. The *federated learner* trains a separate model on each client's data and averages predictions; the *centralized learner* pools all data and trains a single model. We study two estimators: ridgeless (minimum-norm) least squares, which interpolates the training data when $p > n$, and ridge regression with optimally-tuned regularization. We derive the asymptotic prediction risk of both the federated and centralized learners as a function of the overparameterization ratio, the noise level, and the heterogeneity parameters (the angle between cluster means, the ratio of their norms, and their covariance structures).

Our main findings are as follows:

1. **Three regimes of federated learning.** The relative performance of federated and centralized learners is governed by three regimes, determined by where each learner sits relative to its interpolation threshold. When both are underparameterized ($p < n/k$), centralization always wins. When local models have crossed the threshold but the global model has not ($n/k < p < n$), centralization wins massively. When both are overparameterized ($p > n$), federation can outperform centralization — particularly near $p \approx n$, where the centralized model is at its interpolation threshold but local models have moved into the regime of benign overfitting.

2. **Heterogeneity is a second-order effect.** Across all heterogeneity axes we study — directional divergence between client distributions (angle between means), differences in signal strength (norm ratio), and differences in covariance structure — the degree of heterogeneity modulates the *magnitude* of the performance gap between federation and centralization, but does not change *which strategy wins*. The regime boundaries are determined by the overparameterization ratio, not the heterogeneity.

3. **Regularization eliminates the federation advantage.** With optimally-tuned ridge regression, which smooths out the interpolation threshold, centralization consistently outperforms federation across all overparameterization ratios. The regime where federation helps exists only for ridgeless estimators — that is, it is a consequence of the interpolation catastrophe, not of any intrinsic advantage of distributed learning.

These results have direct implications for federated learning practice. They suggest that the extensive effort devoted to developing heterogeneity-robust aggregation algorithms may be addressing a second-order concern. In high-dimensional settings, the first-order lever is *model sizing*: choosing the model complexity $p$ relative to the local and global sample sizes. When this ratio is in a favorable regime, simple federated averaging suffices; when it is not, no amount of heterogeneity-robust aggregation will help.

Concurrent and related work by Rakovic et al. (2023) also studies double descent in the federated setting, deriving closed-form expressions for the excess risk in linear regression. Our work differs in several respects: we provide a systematic characterization across multiple heterogeneity dimensions (rather than focusing on the double descent curve alone), we directly compare ridgeless and ridge estimators to isolate the role of regularization, and we frame our results in terms of actionable guidance for model sizing in FL.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formalizes the setup. Section 3 presents our theoretical results on the risk of federated and centralized learners. Section 4 identifies the three regimes and characterizes the role of the overparameterization ratio. Section 5 studies the effect of heterogeneity across multiple dimensions. Section 6 compares ridgeless and ridge estimators. Section 7 discusses implications for federated learning practice, and Section 8 concludes.
