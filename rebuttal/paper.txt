                                                         Abstract
 1             Datasets in many settings naturally partition into clusters arising from sub-
 2             populations, batch effects, or aggregation across multiple sources. A common
 3             response to such heterogeneity is to ensemble learners trained on each cluster
 4             rather than fit a single model to the pooled data. Prior work motivating
 5             such approaches has typically considered settings in which both the covariate
 6             distribution and the conditional outcome model differ across clusters; the role
 7             of cluster-aware partitioning and ensembling based solely on the covariate
 8             distribution remains to be explored. We address this case for ridge-regularized
 9             least-squares regression under a linear outcome model and consider all ridge
10             penalty values λ ≥ 0, including the special case of the ridgeless predictor at λ = 0.
11             By considering both fixed-effects and random-effects models, we argue that under
12             random effects, an optimally tuned pooled ridge predictor always outperforms
13             ensembles of individually optimally tuned predictors. For fixed effects, we derive
14             a general formula for the pooled and ensembled predictors to characterize the
15             role of both regression coefficients as well as the predictor distribution shifts.
16             Together, these results generalize prior risk analyses of bagging and random-
17             partition estimation using ridge and ridgeless regression predictors from the i.i.d.
18             setting to encompass covariate shift and heterogeneity-aware partition structure.


19   1    Introduction
20   Heterogeneity in the distribution of covariates is a common artifact of modern datasets, where data
21   can be composed of natural sub-populations, clusters, batch effects, and multiple sources [Chauhan
22   et al., 2010, Goh et al., 2017]. A long-standing question in this setting is whether prediction
23   models should explicitly acknowledge this structure or ignore it. This question has a substantial
24   classical literature on generalization across multiple distributions [Ben-David et al., 2010, Crammer
25   et al., 2008, Mansour et al., 2008] and on pooled estimation under heterogeneity [Li et al., 2022,
26   Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2015]. One natural response has been the development of ensembling
27   frameworks, in which the training data is first separated into its component clusters or natural
28   partitions, a learning algorithm is trained independently on each cluster, and the resulting single-
29   cluster predictors are combined using weights that reward cross-cluster generalization on the training
30   set [Deodhar and Ghosh, 2007, Guan et al., 2019, Patil and Parmigiani, 2018, Ramchandran and
31   Mukherjee, 2021, Ramchandran et al., 2020, Trivedi et al., 2015]. Across a range of base learners,
32   including neural networks, random forests, and regularized regression, such ensembles have been
33   shown empirically to generalize more reliably than a single model trained on the pooled data.
34   Throughout the paper, we refer to this strategy simply as ensembling, and contrast it with pooling,
35   which refers to training a single model on the entire training set with the same base learner. More
36   specifically, we compare two procedures built from the same base learner. The pooled procedure
37   trains a single predictor on the pooled training data. The ensemble procedure trains one predictor

39   predictions. Our goal is to determine whether the second strategy offers any advantage when clusters
40   differ only in their covariate distributions and share the same conditional outcome model.
41   Although ensembling has been motivated by settings in which both the covariate distribution and
42   the conditional outcome model vary across sources, it is far less clear what role cluster-aware
43   partitioning plays when the heterogeneity is purely in the covariates. In this paper, we isolate this
44   question by studying a setting with generalized covariate shift but no concept shift. Specifically,
45   we analyze ridge-regularized least-squares regression with outcomes generated by a well-specified
46   linear model whose coefficients are shared across clusters and test points; only the marginal
47   distribution of the covariates varies both within the training set and between train and test sets.

48   1.1   Summary of Contributions

49   Theoretical results. We provide general formulas for the risk of the ensemble and pooled learners
50   using ridge-regularized least-squares regression as the base learner under a well-specified linear
51   outcome model and arbitrary cluster-specific covariate distributions. When the ridge penalty λ > 0,
52   we prove that the pooled predictor has uniformly smaller (asymptotic) prediction risk than the
53   ensemble built on the true cluster partitions. This holds independently of the angles, norms, and
54   covariance structures of the cluster means. When the ridge penalty λ = 0 for the ridgeless
55   predictor, we show that if both learners are in either the underparameterized or overparameterized
56   regime, the pooled predictor similarly outperforms the ensemble; the only point at which this
57   relationship switches is when the pooled predictor is at the interpolation threshold where the total
58   number of samples equals the number of covariates, whereas the base learners constituting the
59   ensemble are in the over-parameterized regime. In this case, the implicit regularization caused by
60   overparameterization allows the ensemble to achieve lower variance than the pooled predictor.

61   Simulation studies across regimes. We confirm our theoretical results with simulations for both
62   ridge and ridgeless predictors that span the underparameterized regime, the interpolation threshold,
63   and the overparameterized regime. In every configuration we consider—varying cluster mean
64   angles, cluster mean norms, and cluster covariance structures—the pooled predictor matches or
65   outperforms the ensemble when λ > 0 (ridge regression). For ridgeless regression at λ = 0, the
66   simulations confirm the theoretical result that the relationship between approaches depends on the
67   regime, with the pooled learner largely dominating the ensemble when both are under- or over-
68   parameterized. Furthermore, we find that the ensembling-versus-pooling gap is driven by the aspect
69   ratio p/n, as opposed to the covariate distribution or partition strategy.

70   1.2   Related Work

71   Our work builds on two closely related lines of recent research. Hastie et al. [2022] provide the exact
72   asymptotic risk formulas for ridge and ridgeless regression that underpin the qualitative behavior we
73   observe, and Patil et al. [2023] analyze a class of bagging procedures (subagging and splagging) in
74   a supervised setting similar to ours. We defer discussion of the related unsupervised stacked-SVD
75   setting of Baharav et al. [2025] to Appendix A.1, since our focus is on supervised prediction.

76   Asymptotic risk of ridge and ridgeless regression. A line of recent work has produced
77   precise asymptotic risk characterizations for high-dimensional regression in the proportional regime
78   p/n → γ, building on classical random matrix theory. Dobriban and Wager [2018] provide early
79   asymptotic risk formulas for ridge regression and classification in this regime. Hastie et al. [2022]
80   subsequently derive exact formulas for the prediction risk of the minimum-norm (ridgeless) least-
81   squares estimator and of ridge regression under a well-specified linear model with arbitrary covariate
82   covariance. Their formulas make explicit several phenomena central to our experimental story: the
83   ridgeless risk diverges at the interpolation threshold γ = 1 and decreases beyond it (the “double
84   descent” shape, identified empirically by Belkin et al. [2019] and analyzed as benign overfitting by
85   Bartlett et al. [2020]), and even a small ridge penalty smooths the interpolation peak away. We use
86   these results as a direct point of reference when interpreting the risk profile of our two learners.

87   Bagging in overparameterized regression. Patil et al. [2023] characterize the asymptotic
88   prediction risk of two bagging variants for ridge and ridgeless least-squares predictors in the

            96   that properly tuned ridge regression is optimal among the methods considered, with no ensembling
            97   strategy surpassing it. One notable finding is that under isotropic covariates, the optimally-tuned
            98   subagged ridgeless predictor matches the asymptotic risk of optimally-tuned ridge; that is, bagging
            99   implicitly performs the regularization that ridge performs explicitly. This is further illustrated by
           100   comparing ensembles of ridgeless predictors trained on subsets to a single ridgeless predictor trained
           101   on the full dataset: the ensemble shows benefit largely at the full predictor’s interpolation threshold,
           102   where each ensemble component has been pushed into the overparameterized regime and exhibits
           103   variance stabilization.
           104   LeJeune et al. [2020] make this implicit-regularization view explicit, showing that in the proportional
           105   asymptotic regime, ensembles of ordinary least-squares predictors trained on random subsets behave
           106   like a single predictor with an effective ridge penalty determined by the subsample size. In a
           107   complementary fixed-dimension kernel-ridge setting, Zhang et al. [2015] analyze a divide-and-
           108   conquer estimator that partitions the data into K disjoint subsets, fits kernel ridge regression on each,
           109   and averages the predictors; they show that for K not too large, this matches the convergence rate of
           110   the predictor trained on the full dataset. Together, these results show that bagging and partitioning
           111   act as implicit regularization, with primary gains for linear predictors concentrated in regimes
           112   where interpolation would otherwise produce high variance; none, however, exceed optimally tuned
           113   ridge. Our work extends this picture by asking how covariate heterogeneity and heterogeneity-
           114   aware partitioning affect the benefit of ensembling versus pooling: rather than assuming i.i.d.
           115   data with uniformly random partitions, we study a mixture of clusters with heterogeneous
           116   covariate distributions and partition along the underlying cluster structure, investigating whether
           117   the conclusions of Patil et al. [2023] continue to hold.

           230     We conduct a comprehensive simulation study to validate our theoretical results. We describe the
           231     common setup, then present results for ridge and ridgeless estimators with a particular focus on the
           232     effects of cluster heterogeneity. All experiments were run using 4 CPU cores on a local machine.


           233     3.1                                      Simulation Setup

           234     We simulate n = 2000 total training observations partitioned into K = 2 equal-sized clusters with
           235     n1 = n2 = 1000. Within each cluster ℓ ∈ {1, 2}, the covariates are independent draws from a
           236     multivariate normal distribution with cluster-specific mean and identity covariance: Xℓ ∼ Np (µℓ , I)
           237     where µℓ ∈ Rp , so that all covariates are uncorrelated within and across clusters and have unit
           238     marginal variance. The first cluster’s mean µ1 is a unit-norm random vector drawn from Np (0, I)
           239     and rescaled so that ∥µ1 ∥ = 1; the second cluster’s mean µ2 is constructed via Gram-Schmidt
           240     orthogonalization to have a prescribed angle with µ1 and a prescribed norm (the specific values used
           241     are described in Section 3.2). The means µ1 and µ2 are redrawn independently in every repetition.
           242     The coefficient vector β ∈ Rp used to generate the outcome is drawn from Np (0, I) and normalized
           243     to unit norm (∥β∥ = 1); the same β is used to generate training and test outcomes within each
           244     repetition. For cluster ℓ ∈ {1, 2} and samples i = 1, . . . , nℓ , training data is generated
                                                                                                            √ as Xℓ ∼
           245     Np (µℓ , I) with response Yℓ = β ⊤ X ℓ + εℓ , where εℓ ∼ Nnℓ (0, σ 2 I) and σ = 1/ 5, giving a
           246     signal-to-noise ratio SNR = ∥β∥2 /σ 2 = 5 (matching the highest SNR considered by [Hastie et al.,
           247     2022]).
           248     The test data X ⋆ ∈ Rntest ×p is drawn from a standard multivariate normal; X ⋆ ∼
           249     Np (0, I), with ntest = 1000. The outcome is simulated with no noise, Y ⋆ = X ⋆ β,
           250     isolating the prediction risk of each estimator on a fixed reference distribution and matching
           251     the theoretical setup of [Hastie et al., 2022]. The covariate dimension is varied across
           252     p ∈ {250, 500, 750, 1050, 1500, 2050, 3000, 4000, 5000, 6000}, spanning the underparameterized
           253     regime, the cluster interpolation threshold at p = n/K = 1000, the Pooled interpolation threshold
           254     at p = n = 2000, and the deep overparameterized regime. We avoid values of p that exactly
           255     coincide with these thresholds to prevent numerical instability at the singular boundary. All results
           256     are averaged over 100 independent repetitions, with 95% confidence intervals computed from the
           257     t-distribution.

           280   4    Discussion
           281   This paper presents a precise asymptotic comparison between pooled and ensemble learners fit with
           282   ridge-regularized linear regression in a proportional asymptotic regime. Our results demonstrate the
           283   benefits of pooled methods in random-effects settings and quantify the precise efficiency gains over
           284   ensemble learners.
           285   The simulations complement the theory and additionally capture behavior near the interpolation
           286   thresholds for ridgeless regression. For the ridgeless estimator, the MSE ratio shows distinct
           287   patterns across three regimes. When both the ensemble base learners and the pooled learner are
           288   underparameterized (p < nℓ ), the advantage of the pooled approach grows with p, vanishing
           289   only in the fixed-dimensional limit p ≪ nℓ where the two methods perform comparably. At the
           290   ensemble’s interpolation threshold (p ≈ nℓ ), its MSE spikes and drives the ratio sharply up. As
           291   p moves into the intermediate regime nℓ < p < n, the ratio dips slightly below 1 and continues
           292   to drop as p approaches the pooled learner’s interpolation threshold (p ≈ n); at this point each
           293   ensemble base learner is already overparameterized, and the ensemble enjoys its largest advantage;
           294   this complements results from Patil et al. [2023]. Notably, the MSE spike of the ensemble is roughly
           295   a quarter the size of that for the pooled approach, indicating that the former’s worst-case performance
           296   is considerably better than the latter’s. Once both methods are overparameterized (p > n), the ratio
           297   approaches 1 from below as both MSEs converge to the null risk. The picture differs sharply for
           298   ridge regression with λ tuned optimally per learner: no regime favors the ensemble. The MSE ratio
           299   stays between 1.06 and 1.62 across all p, and the raw MSEs are smooth and monotone in p, with no
           300   spikes at either interpolation threshold. As in the ridgeless case, the ratio approaches 1 as p ≫ n,
           301   with both methods converging to the null risk.
           302   Variations in cluster structure have essentially no effect on the comparison. Across all four panels of
           303   Figure 2, the curves for the different configurations overlap so tightly that the differences sit below
           304   the 95% confidence intervals at almost every p. To probe whether this depends on partitioning along
           305   the true cluster structure, we ran an ablation in which data were generated under the two-cluster
           306   framework of Section 3.1 but randomly split into two halves to train the ensemble base learners.
           307   For both ridge and ridgeless regression, the results were identical to Figure 2, even though the
           308   random partitions bore no relation to the true clusters. We further considered a fully non-clustered
           309   regime aligned with Patil et al. [2023], in which both training and test data were drawn from a p-
           310   dimensional standard multivariate normal. Splitting the training data into two random equal halves
           311   for the ensemble, with the pooled learner trained on the full set, gave the same raw MSEs, and
           312   therefore the same MSE ratio, as the clustered experiments. The salient feature of the partition is
           313   not its alignment with any cluster structure but simply that it splits the data into two equal halves so
           314   that 2 × p/nℓ = p/n. The dominant axis of variation is the aspect ratio; the geometry of the cluster
           315   means, and indeed the very presence of covariate heterogeneity, is by comparison irrelevant.

           316   Limitations and future work. Our analysis focuses on well-specified linear models with shared
           317   coefficients and a shared noise variance across clusters. This is the appropriate setting for isolating
           318   pure covariate heterogeneity, but it does not cover model misspecification, nonlinear predictors, or
           319   clusters with different noise levels or signal-to-noise ratios. Several extensions are natural. The
           320   random matrix arguments developed here extend to joint shifts in mean and variance, beyond the
           321   mean shifts we consider. The case of estimated rather than known cluster memberships is open, as
           322   is the move beyond linear regression, which can follow the template of Hastie et al. [2019], where
           323   results were first established under linear models before being extended to general nonlinear settings.
           324   Theoretical analysis of the ridgeless estimator near the interpolation peaks under covariate shift is
           325   an additional future direction. Finally, when cluster effects are fixed rather than random, the optimal
           326   choice between pooled and ensemble strategies depends on unknown cluster parameters, making
           327   adaptive selection an interesting open question.

           395   A     Appendix
           396   A.1       Further Related Work

           397   An unsupervised analog: stacking versus per-source SVD. Baharav et al. [2025] study a
           398   closely related question in an unsupervised setting: given multiple datasets that share a common
           399   right singular subspace but differ in signal strength and noise level, they investigate whether it is
           400   better to stack all datasets and run a single SVD (stacked SVD) or to run SVD on each dataset
           401   separately and aggregate the per-source estimates (stacked-SVDs). Their main finding is that the
           402   stacked SVD approach dominates when dataset-specific weights are chosen optimally (with optimal
           403   weights downweighting noisier or lower-signal sources), but that without optimal weighting, neither
           404   approach dominates universally and per-source aggregation can occasionally win. Their setting
           405   differs from ours in the source of heterogeneity: all of their datasets share the same isotropic noise
           406   structure and signal direction but differ in signal-to-noise ratio, whereas in our supervised setting
           407   all clusters share the same signal and noise level but differ in their covariate distributions. Despite
           408   this difference, the two settings raise the same fundamental question: when multiple data sources
           409   differ in some structural way, is it optimal to combine them before fitting or to fit separately and
           410   aggregate?
